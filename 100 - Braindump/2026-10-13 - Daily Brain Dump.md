Part II — Impersonation Research: Working Definitions

The research focused on finding examples that might relate to impersonation, mimicry, voice imitation, or the presentation of multiple identities.

These concepts should remain distinct.

A. Voice resemblance

A person reportedly hears a voice that sounds like someone they know.

This could be relevant to mimicry research, but resemblance alone does not establish that another entity deliberately copied the person.

B. Apparent impersonation

A voice or response is interpreted as presenting itself as a particular person or identity.

The key question is whether the source material supports that interpretation or whether the identity was suggested by the investigators.

C. Explicit identity claim

A communication appears to identify itself as a named person or entity.

The exact question, response, communication method, and surrounding conversation must be recorded. A one-word response may be ambiguous.

D. Multiple identities / disguise

A claim that one presence can appear as, speak as, or present itself through multiple identities.

This is broader than a single instance of voice mimicry and may require different evidence.

E. Puppeteer / control hypothesis

A separate possibility is that one entity controls, directs, or manipulates multiple other entities or identities.

This must not automatically be treated as equivalent to impersonation. One entity pretending to be several identities and one entity controlling several independent entities are different hypotheses.

Research rule: These categories can overlap, but they must not be merged simply because they share names, voices, or themes.

Part IX — Research Decisions and Methodological Notes

The research should begin with claims, not conclusions

The purpose of this stage is to locate and classify potential examples. It is not to prove in advance that impersonation is occurring.

Preserve the full question-response sequence

For each communication, the eventual index should capture:

- Investigation and publication date.
- Timestamp.
- Exact preceding question.
- Exact reported response.
- Communication method.
- Speaker attribution, if known.
- Whether the claim comes from direct footage, a transcript, a later interview, or a vault interpretation.
- The interpretation proposed by the investigators.
- Alternative explanations.
- Contradictory responses elsewhere in the same investigation.

Keep the source types distinct

The Cresson transcript is an external transcript of an investigation video. The Farrar and Conjuring House examples come from a later interview transcript. The Lucy / Marys material comes from historical GSNC notes.

These sources do not all provide the same kind of evidence.

A retrospective interview can preserve a witness report without independently verifying the original event. A vault note can establish that a theory was proposed without establishing that the theory is correct.

Do not merge impersonation with control

A possible mimic, a source claiming a particular identity, a source disguising itself, and a puppeteer controlling several entities are related research questions but not interchangeable claims.

The RED JOHN / Puppeteer hypothesis should remain separate unless a specific source establishes a meaningful connection.
