Discovery A — “You know” in identity-related questions

A potentially interesting phrase family appears in several investigations:

- Villisca Axe Murder House — December 5, 2021: After questions such as “Who are we speaking with?” and “Please tell us who you are,” the response is recorded as “You know,” twice.
- Hotel Del Coronado — October 26, 2021: In response to a request for a name, the phrase “You know me” appears.
- Stanley Hotel Return — April 16, 2022: “You really know” appears in response to “Who are you?”
- Farrar Elementary — October 2024: A name-related question is followed by “You know.”
- Cresson — August 2, 2026: “You know” appears in questions concerning the most powerful presence and a possible mimic behind Celina.

Working interpretation

The recurring structure may be more interesting than the words alone:

Investigator asks who or what a presence is → response implies the investigator already knows.

Potential explanations include:

- A recurring phrase or communication pattern.
- A response shaped by the wording of the question.
- Similar interpretive expectations across investigations.
- A potentially meaningful pattern within the GSNC archive.

It is not evidence by itself that the same entity communicated in all these places.

Follow-up: Record the exact question, response, communication method, and timestamp for every occurrence. Separate the exact phrase “You know” from related phrases such as “You know me.”

Discovery B — Following versus attachment

The following question appears in several different contexts:

- Shanley Hotel — August 1, 2021: “Are you gonna follow us for the rest of the night?” receives a reported “Yes.”
- Wilson Castle — August 8, 2021: Steve Brodt reportedly describes an entity named Seven as following him.
- Conjuring House Alone — October 31, 2021: A soldier is reportedly associated with following the investigators back.
- Stanley Hotel Return — April 2022: Sam asks whether anyone is following them; the response is unclear.
- Haunted Factory — April 12, 2026: Sam asks whether a presence upstairs is also the person following them all night; “Don’t go looking” is recorded.
- Cresson — August 2, 2026: “Followed you” appears in the communication, but a direct question about whether a spirit followed the investigators onto the property receives “No.”
- Corvin Castle — October 4, 2026: The question “Will you follow us around?” receives “No.”

Working interpretation

Following and attachment should be tracked separately.

A reported presence following someone does not automatically mean it is attached to them. Similarly, a “No” in one investigation does not negate a possible following pattern in another.

A useful future comparison is:

1. Presence reported at a location.
2. Presence reportedly follows someone away from that location.
3. Repeated encounters occur elsewhere.
4. Someone interprets the pattern as attachment.
5. Attempts are made to remove, banish, or cleanse the alleged presence.

This is a proposed research structure, not an established mechanism.

The word “Me” and variants such as “It’s me,” “Not me,” and “That’s me” appear in multiple investigations, including Conjuring House, Queen Mary, Hotel Del Coronado, Villisca, Stanley Hotel Return, Haunted Factory, Farrar, and Corvin.

Some particularly useful contrasts:

- Haunted Factory: “Me” appears after a question about whether a presence downstairs is now with the investigators.

- Hotel Del Coronado: “It’s me,” “Not me,” and “That’s me” appear amid identity and death-related questions.

Working interpretation

Counting every occurrence of “Me” as one phenomenon would lose important context.

The word might function as:

- A response to a greeting.
- A claim of identity.
- A claim of presence.
- A response to a question about a previous encounter.
- A denial or correction.
- An ambiguous fragment whose meaning cannot be resolved.

The future index should record the question immediately before each response and classify its function only after preserving the exact wording.

Part IV — Semantic-Field Lists: New Research Workflow

The user became excited about the number of potentially overlooked patterns and decided to create semantic-field lists.

The aim is to organize related words and concepts so that future comparisons do not depend only on remembering a phrase or searching for one exact spelling.

Proposed semantic fields

1. Confinement and freedom

Trapped, stuck, imprisoned, locked, wall, hole, buried, stay, leave, escape, free, freedom, release, home.

2. Identity, recognition, and mimicry

Me, it’s me, you know, you know me, name, mask, not me, that’s not my name, mimic, copy, pretend, someone else.

3. Following, attachment, and movement

Follow, followed, come with, go with, stay with, leave with, return, never left, came back, here, there, upstairs, downstairs.

4. Hierarchy and control

King, master, leader, boss, highest, powerful, control, command, obey, puppeteer, overseer, multiple, separate.

5. Communication, permission, and refusal

Yes, no, maybe, stop, go, leave, don’t, can’t, won’t, answer, speak, listen, help, please.

6. Death, afterlife, and physical remains

Dead, death, died, murder, killed, blood, body, corpse, buried, bones, hell, heaven, damned, pain.

7. Recognition, memory, and continuity

Remember, know, knew, recognize, before, again, always, never, still, returned, never left, I was there.

Rules for using the lists

- Record the exact response first; categorize it second.
- One response may belong to multiple semantic fields.
- Keep communication methods separate: Estes, spirit box, Ovilus, EVP, investigator speech, flashlight, EMF, and other equipment observations should not be treated as interchangeable.
- Preserve uncertainty in transcription, including words that only sound like a particular phrase.
- Track contradictions, not just matches.
- Include an “outliers / unclear” category. Do not force words such as “Mask,” “Nineteen,” or “Amica” into a convenient field when their meaning is uncertain.
- Keep exact phrase matches separate from broader semantic similarities.
- Count distinct investigations as well as raw occurrences; a phrase repeated ten times in one session is not the same as a phrase independently appearing in ten investigations.
- Preserve the exact preceding question whenever possible.

Part V — Recommended Index for the Next Pass

A future cross-investigation index should ideally record:

|   |   |
|---|---|
|Field|Purpose|
|Exact response|Preserves the source wording|
|Investigation and date|Establishes chronology|
|Timestamp|Makes the source easy to revisit|
|Communication method|Separates different devices and techniques|
|Exact preceding question|Preserves context|
|Investigator asking|Helps compare question wording|
|Response type|Word, phrase, unclear sound, or equipment event|
|Semantic field|Allows broad thematic searches|
|Immediate interpretation|Records what the original note says|
|Other matching investigations|Identifies possible repetitions|
|Confidence / uncertainty|Prevents ambiguous material from being overstated|

Three separate outputs would be particularly useful:

1. Exact repeated phrases — identical or near-identical wording.
2. Repeated question-response structures — similar questions followed by similar responses.
3. Semantic-field patterns — different words that may perform a similar function.

The most promising initial leads to check first are “You know” in identity-related questions, following questions and their contrasting answers, and the context-dependent uses of “Me.”
